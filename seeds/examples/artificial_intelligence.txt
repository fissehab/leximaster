# Conversation examples: artificial_intelligence
artificial intelligence
- [the field] Artificial intelligence studies how to build systems that perceive, reason, learn, and act.
- [history of the term] John McCarthy coined the term "artificial intelligence" for the 1956 Dartmouth workshop.
- [explaining to a friend] Most of what people call artificial intelligence today is really machine learning models trained on lots of data.

AI
- [the field, abbreviation] AI has gone through several cycles of hype and disappointment since the 1950s.
- [a deployed system] Our support team uses an AI to draft replies, but a person reviews every one.
- [executive meeting] Before we add AI to the product, let's agree on what problem it's actually solving.

machine intelligence
- [general capability] Some researchers prefer "machine intelligence" because it sounds less like a marketing term.
- [in a paper] We treat machine intelligence as the ability to achieve goals across a range of environments.

artificial general intelligence
- [hypothetical system] Artificial general intelligence refers to a system that could learn and perform most intellectual tasks a human can.
- [in a debate] Nobody in the room could agree on a test that would tell us we'd reached artificial general intelligence.
- [explaining to a manager] Our forecasting model is narrow; it's nowhere near artificial general intelligence.

AGI
- [hypothetical system] AGI is usually contrasted with narrow AI, which is built for one task or domain.
- [skeptical conversation] Every few months someone claims AGI is two years away, and the definition keeps shifting.
- [in a strategy review] Our roadmap doesn't depend on AGI; it depends on better data and evaluation.

superintelligence
- [hypothetical concept] Superintelligence describes a hypothetical system that far exceeds human ability in nearly every domain.
- [safety discussion] Debates about superintelligence often focus on whether its goals would stay aligned with ours.

narrow AI
- [task-specific system] A spam filter is a good example of narrow AI: excellent at one task, useless at others.
- [explaining to executives] Everything we've deployed so far is narrow AI, trained for a specific business problem.
- [in a blog post] Narrow AI can beat experts at chess and still fail at tasks a child finds trivial.

strong AI
- [philosophy of mind] In philosophy of mind, strong AI is the claim that a properly programmed computer actually has a mind.
- [general capability, loose use] People sometimes use strong AI as a synonym for artificial general intelligence.
- [in a seminar] The Chinese room argument was aimed squarely at strong AI, not at useful software.

weak AI
- [philosophy of mind] Weak AI claims only that computers can simulate thinking, leaving open whether they actually understand anything.
- [task-specific, loose use] In casual use, weak AI just means a system built for a single, narrow task.

Turing test
- [historical test] Alan Turing proposed what we now call the Turing test in his 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence.
- [how it works] In the Turing test, a judge chats with a human and a machine and tries to tell which is which.
- [in a discussion] Passing a casual Turing test says more about fluent text than about real understanding.

Chinese room
- [thought experiment] The Chinese room argument, published in 1980, says symbol manipulation alone isn't enough for understanding.
- [debating language models] Critics invoke the Chinese room when they argue that chatbots process text without grasping meaning.
- [at dinner with friends] The Chinese room imagines a person following rules to answer in Chinese without knowing any Chinese.

symbolic AI
- [rule-based approach] Symbolic AI represents knowledge with explicit symbols and rules and reasons by manipulating them.
- [historical context] Symbolic AI dominated the field from the 1950s through the 1980s, before statistical methods took over.
- [design review] For the compliance checks, a bit of symbolic AI is easier to audit than a neural network.

good old-fashioned AI
- [symbolic tradition] John Haugeland coined "good old-fashioned AI" to describe the symbolic, logic-based approach.
- [team meeting, informal] The scheduler is good old-fashioned AI: a constraint solver and a handful of rules.

connectionism
- [approach to cognition] Connectionism models cognition as patterns of activity across networks of simple, interconnected units.
- [history] The 1986 work on parallel distributed processing helped revive connectionism after years of skepticism.
- [in a paper] Modern deep learning is in many ways the engineering descendant of connectionism.

connectionist
- [describing a model] A connectionist model learns by adjusting connection weights rather than by following hand-written rules.

cybernetics

- [feedback loops] Ideas from cybernetics, like feedback and self-regulation, show up throughout control theory and robotics.

expert system

heuristic search
- [guided search] Heuristic search uses an estimate of the remaining cost to decide which node to expand next.

- [search strategy] Best-first search always expands the node that the evaluation function rates as most promising.

- [search strategy] Breadth-first search explores all nodes at one depth before moving to the next level.

A* search
- [search algorithm] A* search ranks nodes by f(n) = g(n) + h(n), the path cost so far plus a heuristic estimate.

- [performance debugging] Our kernel wasn't using the tensor cores at all, which explained the poor throughput.

matrix multiplication
- [core operation] Most of a transformer's compute goes into matrix multiplication.
- [why gpus help] GPUs excel at matrix multiplication because the work splits into many independent pieces that run in parallel.
- [explaining to a friend] Matrix multiplication combines rows and columns of numbers; a model does billions of multiply-add steps for every word it writes.

floating point
- [number format] Floating point numbers trade exactness for range, which can cause small rounding errors.
- [memory savings] Storing weights in 16-bit floating point halves memory compared with 32-bit.
- [debugging nondeterminism] Floating point nondeterminism on GPUs explains why identical runs gave slightly different results.

mixed precision
- [basic technique] Mixed precision training does most computation in 16-bit while keeping a 32-bit master copy of the weights.
- [loss scaling] With FP16 mixed precision, loss scaling keeps small gradients from underflowing to zero.
- [team meeting] Turning on mixed precision nearly doubled our training speed with no loss in accuracy.

memory bandwidth
- [basic definition] Memory bandwidth is how fast data moves between GPU memory and the compute units.
- [decoding bottleneck] Token-by-token generation is usually limited by memory bandwidth, not raw compute.
- [hardware choice] For inference, memory bandwidth often matters more than peak FLOPS.

on-device AI
- [basic definition] On-device AI runs models directly on phones or laptops instead of in the cloud.
- [privacy benefit] On-device AI keeps personal data local and works without an internet connection.
- [hardware constraint] On-device AI forces us to use small, quantized models because of memory and battery limits.

edge AI
- [basic definition] Edge AI runs inference close to where data is created, like on cameras, sensors, or factory equipment.
- [latency benefit] Edge AI avoids round trips to the cloud, which matters for real-time control.
- [operations challenge] The edge AI deployment needs a plan for updating models on thousands of devices.

cloud inference
- [basic definition] With cloud inference, the model runs on a provider's servers and the app sends requests over the network.
- [access trade-off] Cloud inference gives us access to bigger models but adds latency and data-sharing concerns.
- [cost model] Cloud inference costs scale with usage, so heavy workloads can get expensive.

serving
- [running models in production] Serving a 70-billion-parameter model takes several GPUs and careful batching.
- [serving infrastructure] Our serving stack handles autoscaling, batching, and request routing.
- [everyday: food portion] One serving of pasta is smaller than most people think.

model serving
- [basic definition] Model serving is the infrastructure that hosts a trained model and answers prediction requests in production.
- [cost shift] For us, model serving now costs more than training.
- [design review] The model serving layer should support rolling back to the previous version within minutes.

API
- [programming interface] The API takes a JSON request and returns the model's response.
- [team meeting] Are we calling the API directly or going through our internal gateway?
- [business model] Selling access through an API lets customers build on our models without ever seeing the weights.

endpoint
- [api address] Each model version gets its own endpoint, so clients can pin a specific version.
- [deployment behavior] The inference endpoint scales to zero when there's no traffic.
- [clinical trials sense] The trial's primary endpoint was overall survival.
- [security sense] Every endpoint, including employee laptops, needs the security agent installed.

rate limit
- [api constraint] We hit the rate limit during the batch job, so add retries with exponential backoff.
- [abuse protection] A rate limit protects the service from abuse and keeps costs predictable.
- [team meeting] Can we get the rate limit raised before the launch?

token budget
- [cost cap] Each request has a token budget of four thousand, including the retrieved context.
- [reasoning budget] Raising the token budget for thinking improved accuracy on the hardest problems.
- [agent stopping rule] The agent stops when it runs out of its token budget, even if the task isn't finished.

MLOps
- [basic definition] MLOps applies DevOps practices to machine learning: versioning, automated testing, deployment, and monitoring.
- [team ownership] Our MLOps team owns the training pipelines and the model registry.
- [operational maturity] Without MLOps, every model update is a manual, risky process.

model registry
- [basic definition] A model registry stores versioned models with their metadata, metrics, and approval status.
- [deployment gate] Only models marked "approved" in the model registry can be deployed to production.
- [team meeting] Register the new checkpoint in the model registry with its eval results attached.

pipeline
- [training pipeline] The training pipeline pulls data, cleans it, trains, evaluates, and registers the model.
- [inference pipeline] Our RAG pipeline has three stages: retrieve, rerank, and generate.
- [pipeline parallelism] Pipeline parallelism splits the model's layers across GPUs in sequential stages.
- [everyday: sales] The sales pipeline looks strong for next quarter.

deployment
- [releasing a model] The deployment went smoothly, and the new model now serves all traffic.
- [canary strategy] We use canary deployment: a small share of traffic goes to the new model first.
- [context of use] Deployment context matters; the same model can be low risk in one product and high risk in another.
- [everyday: military] Her deployment overseas lasted nine months.

monitoring
- [production health] Monitoring tracks latency, error rates, and output quality once the model is live.
- [safety monitoring] We use automated monitoring to flag possible misuse in API traffic.

observability
- [basic definition] Observability means you can understand a system's internal state from its logs, metrics, and traces.
- [llm applications] For LLM apps, observability includes logging prompts, tool calls, and token usage per request.

drift detection
- [basic definition] Drift detection flags when production inputs or predictions move away from what the model saw in training.
- [statistical method] Our drift detection compares weekly feature distributions with the training set using a statistical test.
- [response process] When drift detection fires, we investigate before retraining anything.

retraining
- [basic process] Retraining updates the model on fresh data so it keeps up with changing patterns.
- [retraining schedule] Retraining happens monthly, or sooner if drift detection fires.
- [regression risk] Retraining can introduce regressions, so the new model must pass the same evals as the old one.

versioning
- [models and data] Versioning models, data, and prompts together is the most reliable way to reproduce a past result.
- [api versioning] API versioning lets clients upgrade on their own schedule.
- [team meeting] Our prompt versioning was a mess; nobody knew which prompt was in production.

reproducibility
- [research standard] Reproducibility means another team can get the same results using the same code, data, and settings.
- [practical limits] Fixed seeds help reproducibility, but GPU nondeterminism can still cause small differences.
- [field-wide problem] Missing details on data and hyperparameters have hurt reproducibility in many ML papers.
